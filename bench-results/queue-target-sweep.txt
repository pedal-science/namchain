Output-queue target sweep — 2026-09-14, `cb5cc19`+working tree, SSL 2+ Mk II (hw:2,0)
========================================================================================

What was asked
--------------
The knob is the latency-against-dropout trade, and it had to be measured before it could be
defaulted (plan.md §9.5 item 7, §14). The question is not "does a queue help" -- that is arithmetic
-- but "how much latency buys how much dropout tolerance, on this host, with this device", and
"what does the knob do when it is set wrong".

Setup
-----
4 x A2-Full (A2PlanarFull), q = 64 frames (1333.3 us), 48 kHz, 8 capture / 6 playback channels,
`--self-oracle --oracle-window 5`, `SCHED_FIFO 80` + `--cpu 3` applied by the program itself, 300 s
per run (one run is 90 s, marked). Non-RT kernel 7.0.0-1017-raspi, governor `performance`,
`throttled=0x0`, load ~0.3. Every run has a `.txt` report, an `.env` with the machine state before
and after, and a `.heartbeat`.

The numbers
-----------
"queue" is the playback ring's contents sampled once per block, in periods of 64 frames. `min` is the
worst instant of the run -- the stall the device could have absorbed at that moment -- `last` is where
the run ended, which is the one that says whether the queue held or decayed. `underruns` are playback
xruns (the ring ran dry: the event this knob exists to prevent), `overruns` are capture xruns (the
device produced a period the loop had not read: an output queue cannot help, and it is reported
separately for exactly that reason).

  target2             4     2    0.00 .. 3.88    0.95       6         0       17 (17)      11     38.9    9

  p99%  = block p99 as a percentage of the 1333.3 us deadline (gate 50)
  ovr   = blocks whose total exceeded the deadline
  drops(target) = periods the loop refused to write; the bracket is how many of them the *cap* refused
                  rather than the ring's own capacity

What it says
------------

1. **The ring size is the cushion, and it is not latency.** Going from a 4-period to a 6-period ring
   at no cap took playback underruns from 7 to 1 in five minutes and drops from 9 to 0, with the same
   period size and the same queue *level* at the end (2.00 vs 3.12 periods). The ring is not in the
   signal path; only its contents are. It is the headroom a burst needs in order to accumulate instead
   of hitting a ceiling. The 4-period ring was never too small for the audio, it was too small for the
   *recovery*: its ceiling (4 periods) sat close to the level the queue naturally reached (2-3.3), so
   every burst that arrived near the top was refused -- and a refusal is a period of audio lost.

2. **The cap bounds the latency and should not be set below the level the queue reaches on its own.**
   At ring 6 the queue accumulates to ~3.1-3.3 periods by itself. Capping at 4 held it at 3.25 and
   3.52 max (4.7 ms worst case) against 5.88 periods (7.8 ms) unbounded, with no measured underrun
   cost (0 against 1, which is inside the noise of a 5-minute run). Capping at 2 -- *below* the level
   the ring reaches -- produced 25 drops and 9 underruns, the worst of the six configurations. A cap
   under the natural level does not trade latency for tolerance; it manufactures drops, and each drop
   makes the ring shallower for the next burst.

3. **A cap at one period is a positive-feedback failure, not a tight margin.** `target1` underran 2620
   times in 90 seconds -- 29 per second -- with 2696 refusals. The reason is quantisation: a 64-frame
   period is smaller than the device's own consumption step (48-frame USB frames at 48 kHz), so the
   pre-write sample is nearly always *above* a one-period cap even in a healthy stream. The loop
   therefore refuses, the ring drains, the driver underruns, and the recovery re-primes into the same
   cap. The practical floor is not the period size; it is the period size plus the device's step.

4. **The zero-xrun run still had 30 deadline overruns, and that is the whole point.** `ring6-cap4`
   absorbed 30 blocks that exceeded the 1333.3 us deadline (block max 2.2 ms = 1.6 periods) and still
   never starved the device, because the queue held it 3.25 periods deep. This is the trade in the
   cleanest possible form: the host still takes the CPU away, and the queue is what turns that from a
   dropout into nothing. Every previous configuration had deadline overruns *become* xruns; this one
   separated the two.

5. **The two bugs this measurement found are both worth more than the numbers.** A cap applied to a
   stream that is not running starves it of the write that would report and recover the xrun: the
   pointers freeze, "occupancy" reads full, and the loop refuses for ever while the report claims zero
   underruns (measured: 2285 consecutive refusals, one real underrun, a report saying none). And the
   target has to be established immediately before the timed loop, because the bring-up path spends a
   period of the primed queue and the ring's contents are conserved -- a queue that starts a period
   short stays a period short for the whole run. Both are in the code with the measurements that
   found them.

Recommendation
--------------
`--periods 6 --output-queue-periods 4`: 0 xruns and 0 drops over five minutes with a queue holding
~3.25 periods (4.3 ms) and a worst case bounded at 3.52 (4.7 ms). Against the current 4-period ring
and no cap that is 7 fewer playback underruns per five minutes for ~2 ms of extra monitoring latency,
and it is the first configuration in this project's history with a zero-xrun device soak.

Caveats, stated rather than buried
----------------------------------
- Five minutes each. The no-cap rate is ~1.4 underruns/min, so "0 in 5 min" is roughly e^-7 evidence,
  not proof; an hour at `ring6-cap4` would settle it, and that is the next run to take.
- `queue min` reads 0.00 in every run that had an underrun, which makes the floor useless as a
  statistic there: once the stream has starved, the minimum over the run is 0 by definition. The
  honest instrument is the *distribution* of the occupancy (p1/p50) and the depth at the instant a
  stall begins, neither of which exists yet. `last` and `max` are what carry the argument above.
- One host, one device, one period size. The quantisation floor in point 3 is a property of a 64-frame
  period on high-speed USB, and a 48-frame period (1 ms) would have a different one.
- The cap costs a `SNDRV_PCM_IOCTL_STATUS` per period when it is set; the default path is unchanged.
  The render path stayed clean: 0 allocations, 0 frees, in every run.
